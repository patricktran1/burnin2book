# INTRODUCTION

## Fourteen Centimeters

Fourteen centimeters.

In most contexts, it is an unremarkable measurement. About the length of a pencil. Something you could span with your hand.

Inside the abdomen of a child who was not yet two years old, it was enormous.

I noticed that one side of my son Adrian’s abdomen looked different from the other. He seemed perfectly healthy otherwise. He was running around, playing, climbing on things, and behaving exactly like a toddler who had no reason to believe there was anything wrong with him.

Still, the asymmetry bothered me.

I was a physician, which in this situation was both helpful and terrible. Medical training gives you a vocabulary for things you would sometimes prefer not to understand. When something looks abnormal, your mind starts generating possibilities. Most are benign. A few are not.

I wanted the benign possibilities.

Instead, imaging showed a mass in his abdomen measuring approximately fourteen centimeters.

There are moments when time behaves strangely. A few seconds contain an entire future.

I looked at the images and understood enough to be afraid.

The mass was eventually diagnosed as clear cell sarcoma of the kidney, an extremely rare pediatric kidney cancer. Within a short period of time, my son went from being a healthy toddler to becoming a cancer patient.

He needed major surgery. The tumor was removed, but his recovery was complicated by an intussusception, in which part of the bowel telescopes into another section and causes an obstruction. That meant another operation. After surgery came radiation, followed by months of chemotherapy.

I had spent most of my adult life learning medicine, yet suddenly I was on the other side of it.

I knew what a central line was. I understood why a fever during chemotherapy could become an emergency. I knew why blood counts mattered and why a child with a severely suppressed immune system could end up hospitalized because of an infection that another child might shake off at home.

Knowledge did not make any of it easier.

In some ways, it made the uncertainty worse.

Medicine had trained me to solve problems. Examine the patient. Gather data. Generate a differential diagnosis. Order the appropriate tests. Make a treatment plan. Reassess.

There is great comfort in the idea that if you understand a problem well enough, you can control it.

Then your child gets cancer.

Suddenly, you discover the boundary between knowledge and control.

I could read everything ever published about his disease. I could understand the treatment protocol. I could talk with physicians and ask informed questions. I could advocate for him and make sure nothing important was missed.

What I could not do was guarantee that my son would live.

That was unbearable.

It was also one of the first times in my life when achievement stopped working as a defense.

### The Achievement Machine

I had been good at achieving things for as long as I could remember.

I grew up in a family where education mattered. My parents were Vietnamese immigrants who built careers in healthcare after coming to the United States. Medicine ran through my family tree, and science came naturally to me.

I went to Berkeley.

Then medical school at the University of Michigan.

Then surgical training.

Then dermatology.

Then fellowship.

For years, there was always another mountain conveniently waiting behind the one I had just climbed.

That structure suited me.

Give me an objective and I will work backward from it.

Tell me there are a thousand pages to learn and I will make a study schedule.

Tell me a specialty is competitive and I will figure out how to become competitive.

Put a difficult problem in front of me and my natural response is not to retreat. I lean forward.

That trait has served me extraordinarily well.

It has also gotten me into trouble.

There is a point at which confidence becomes overconfidence, action becomes compulsion, and ambition becomes identity. The qualities that make someone successful can become dangerous when they are applied indiscriminately.

A hammer is a useful tool.

It should not become your response to every object in the house.

For much of my life, achievement was my hammer.

If I was unhappy, I looked for another goal.

If I felt uncertain, I gathered more information.

If I felt behind, I worked harder.

If I wanted freedom, I tried to create more money.

If I wanted control, I built more systems.

If life felt uncomfortable, I improved something.

This works remarkably well until you encounter something that cannot be outworked.

Cancer was one of those things.

It forced me to consider a possibility that had barely occurred to me before: perhaps a meaningful life was not simply the result of solving enough problems.

### The First Version of Burn In

That question eventually became the first edition of this book.

I began thinking seriously about burnout, particularly among physicians. Medicine is an almost perfect laboratory for studying high achievement.

The profession selects people who are capable of delaying gratification for absurd lengths of time.

Study now.

Enjoy yourself later.

Work hard in high school so you can get into a good college.

Work hard in college so you can get into medical school.

Work hard in medical school so you can match into residency.

Survive residency so you can finish fellowship.

Finish fellowship so you can finally become an attending physician.

Then you arrive.

And sometimes you look around and think:

*This is it?*

Founders know a version of this feeling.

So do lawyers, executives, athletes, academics, creators, and anyone else who has spent years organizing life around the next milestone.

You tell yourself that the next achievement will change something fundamental.

When the company reaches a certain valuation, you will relax.

When you make partner, you will have arrived.

When you get promoted, you will finally feel secure.

When the business generates enough money, you will have freedom.

When you buy the house, lose the weight, publish the book, get the title, sell the company, or reach the number, you will finally be able to exhale.

Then the milestone comes.

You feel something.

Sometimes it lasts an hour.

Sometimes a week.

Then your mind quietly moves the finish line.

The first *Burn In* was my attempt to understand that cycle.

The basic idea was that burnout feels as though life is burning us from the outside in. Jobs, administrators, schedules, financial pressure, family responsibilities, bureaucracies, expectations, and other people seem to be applying heat from every direction.

My response was to reverse the direction.

Burn in.

Turn inward.

Develop enough awareness, agency, gratitude, and emotional strength that the external world no longer dictates your internal state.

I still believe in that idea.

In fact, I believe in it more strongly today.

But I understand it differently.

### I Was Wrong About Something Important

The first time I wrote this book, I leaned too far into the idea that the solution was internal.

Change your mindset.

Take responsibility.

Let go of resentment.

Stop seeing yourself as a victim.

Choose happiness.

There is truth in all of those ideas.

There is also a dangerous distortion hiding inside them.

Sometimes the outside really is on fire.

Sometimes you are being exploited.

Sometimes the business is failing.

Sometimes the relationship is unhealthy.

Sometimes someone lied to you.

Sometimes you are sick.

Sometimes your brain is not functioning normally.

Sometimes the organization needs to change, not your attitude toward the organization.

Sometimes the correct response to an intolerable situation is not to become more tolerant.

It is to leave.

The distinction matters.

I used to think personal responsibility meant that if I was suffering, I should immediately look inward for the solution.

Today I would put it differently.

Personal responsibility means being willing to see reality accurately enough to decide what to do next.

That may require inner change.

It may require outer change.

Usually, it requires some of both.

Agency is not the belief that everything is your fault.

Agency is the belief that your next move still belongs to you.

I learned that distinction the hard way.

### When the Story Changed

When I wrote the first edition of *Burn In*, I believed I had created substantial financial security through real estate investing.

I thought I had solved one part of the burnout equation.

Money meant options.

Options meant freedom.

Freedom meant I would never again feel trapped by a career, employer, schedule, or circumstance.

That was the theory.

Then parts of the financial world I had built began to unravel.

Someone I had trusted in connection with a number of commercial real estate transactions was eventually convicted of federal bank fraud and sentenced to prison.

Long before I knew exactly how that story would resolve, I became terrified that the consequences would destroy me financially.

I believed I could be exposed to liabilities approaching ten million dollars.

I imagined bankruptcy.

I imagined losing everything.

More importantly, I began to believe that losing everything would prove something about me.

That I had been arrogant.

That I had squandered the opportunities I had been given.

That despite all the degrees, training, work, and achievement, I had ultimately failed.

The financial problem became an identity problem.

Then the identity problem became something much more serious.

I became depressed.

For a long time, I hid how bad it was.

I continued working.

I continued solving problems.

I continued talking to lawyers.

I continued functioning well enough that most people had no idea what was happening inside me.

Eventually, functioning was no longer enough.

There is a chapter later in this book about what happened next.

I will tell you the truth about it.

For now, it is enough to say that I survived something I should not have survived, and afterward I had to reconsider nearly everything I thought I knew about resilience.

Including what I had written in the first version of this book.

### High Performance Has a Blind Spot

There is a particular reason I am writing Version 2.0 for ambitious people.

High performers have tremendous advantages during adversity.

We are resourceful.

We know how to learn.

We tolerate discomfort.

We can delay gratification.

We know how to execute.

We know how to keep moving when other people stop.

These are genuine strengths.

The problem is that strengths create blind spots precisely because they work so often.

If pushing harder has solved ninety percent of the difficult problems in your life, pushing harder becomes automatic.

If you are known as the person who can handle anything, admitting that you cannot handle something becomes psychologically threatening.

If everyone depends on your confidence, uncertainty begins to feel irresponsible.

If success has always restored your self-esteem, failure can feel like more than an event.

It can feel like the disappearance of the person you thought you were.

That is what I did not understand the first time.

Sometimes the highest performer in the room is also the person least likely to admit that the current strategy is killing them.

They can still produce.

Still lead.

Still smile.

Still perform.

The machinery is running.

Nobody sees the temperature gauge.

### Burnout Is Not One Thing

Another thing I understand differently now is the word *burnout* itself.

We use it casually.

"I am burned out."

Sometimes that means someone is exhausted.

Sometimes they hate their job.

Sometimes they have lost control over their schedule.

Sometimes they have been working too many hours for too many months.

Sometimes they are depressed.

Sometimes they are anxious.

Sometimes they are sleeping three hours a night and making increasingly erratic decisions.

Those are not necessarily the same problem.

One danger of labeling every form of suffering "burnout" is that burnout sounds relatively safe.

It sounds occupational.

Temporary.

Fixable with a vacation.

I believed for years that I understood burnout because I had experienced it in surgical training.

At one point during that period, I had even thought about jumping from a parking garage.

In the first edition of this book, I wrote about that thought and then minimized it. I essentially told myself and the reader that it was not "real" suicidality because I did not believe I would actually do it.

Years later, I would jump from a parking garage.

There are sentences from your younger self that become almost unbearable to reread.

That is one of mine.

I include it here because revision should not mean pretending the first version never existed.

The contradiction taught me something.

Being intelligent does not make you objective about yourself.

Medical knowledge did not protect me from rationalizing what was happening in my own mind.

High achievement did not protect me from illness.

Meditation did not make me invulnerable.

Money did not make me safe.

Confidence did not mean I was right.

The lesson was not that all of those things were worthless.

The lesson was that none of them should have been asked to carry the entire weight of a human life.

### The New Question

The first edition asked:

**How do we avoid burning out?**

I think there is a better question now:

**How do we build a life that can hold our fire without being consumed by it?**

Because I do not want you to extinguish your ambition.

I certainly have not extinguished mine.

I still love building.

I love ideas.

I love technology.

I love medicine.

I love creating something where nothing existed before.

I love people who refuse to accept that the current version of the world is the final version.

Ambition is not the enemy.

But ambition needs a container.

Fire inside a fireplace heats a house.

The same fire on the living-room floor burns it down.

What contains ambition?

Self-knowledge.

Health.

Relationships.

Humility.

Financial margin.

Boundaries.

Good systems.

Rest.

Meaning.

The ability to change your mind.

The ability to admit when you are wrong.

People who can tell you the truth.

Enough identity outside your work that one professional disaster does not erase the entire person.

These are not obstacles to high performance.

They are infrastructure for it.

### Burn In 2.0

This book is therefore not about becoming less ambitious.

It is about becoming harder to destroy.

Not invulnerable.

That fantasy caused enough problems.

Resilient.

Flexible.

Connected.

Aware.

Capable of taking a hit without deciding that the hit defines everything that follows.

We will talk about achievement and ego because I know both intimately.

We will talk about money, but not because I am going to teach you how to build a giant real estate portfolio. Money deserves a place in any serious conversation about freedom, but it is a tool, not a verdict on whether your life is working.

We will talk about mindset while being careful not to turn mindset into magical thinking.

We will talk about burnout without pretending that every psychological problem can be solved by changing your attitude.

We will talk about failure.

Shame.

Depression.

Mental illness.

Suicide.

Survival.

Work.

Fatherhood.

Relationships.

Health.

Risk.

Recovery.

And what it means to build again after your own certainty has been shattered.

Some of the ideas from the first edition remain almost exactly as I wrote them.

Keep a beginner's mind.

Be kind.

Think critically for yourself.

I need those principles more now than I did when I first put them on the page.

Perhaps especially the last one.

Think critically about this book too.

You do not need another guru.

You do not need me to tell you exactly how to live.

Take what helps.

Question what does not.

Modify the framework until it fits the life in front of you rather than the life somebody else is selling.

The goal is not to become me.

The goal is to become more fully yourself, without requiring the external world to constantly certify that the person you are becoming is worthy.

### The Fire

I still love the metaphor that gave this book its name.

A small flame is fragile.

A draft can extinguish it.

Feed it, protect it, and give it the right environment, and that flame becomes stronger.

Eventually it can produce warmth and light far beyond itself.

That is the image I had when I first wrote about burning in.

I understand the metaphor differently now.

The goal is not to build an infinitely large fire.

Too much fire destroys.

The goal is to learn what your fire is for.

To know when it needs fuel.

To know when it needs air.

To know when it is spreading somewhere it should not.

To know when someone else's fire is burning through your walls.

To know when you need help putting something out.

To know which parts of your life deserve heat and which deserve quiet.

That is what I want to explore with you.

Not how to escape your life.

Not how to win every game.

Not how to become permanently happy.

How to remain present in a life that will inevitably contain victories, losses, uncertainty, love, pain, mistakes, beauty, consequences, and second chances.

How to build a life you can stay in.

Fourteen centimeters began the first version of this story.

I thought my son's cancer had taught me everything I needed to know about uncertainty.

It had taught me a great deal.

Life had more to teach me.

This is the version of the book I had to survive to write.
