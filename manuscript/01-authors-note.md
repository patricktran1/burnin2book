# Author's Note

I wrote the first edition of this book in the summer of 2021, in a house with moving boxes still on the kitchen floor, in the hours before my son woke up. At thirty-two I had a toddler in the middle of chemotherapy, a surgical practice booked out for months, a growing collection of buildings with corporate tenants, and a theory about all of it. The theory was that burnout came from the outside and the answer came from the inside: change your mindset, take responsibility, build enough financial freedom that nobody could make you do anything, and the heat would stop reaching you. I believed it. I thought I had the receipts.

This edition exists because the years after that book tested the theory in ways I couldn't have designed and wouldn't have chosen. Some of the receipts turned out to be worth less than I'd been told. A man I had trusted in business was later convicted of federal bank fraud and sent to prison. For a long stretch I believed I might be facing nearly ten million dollars in personal exposure, and I became more depressed than I let anyone see. One night I went to the roof of a parking garage and jumped, and I didn't die. I've written about all of that here as plainly as I know how, without the details that would help nobody, because the alternative was a book that pretends the first one was right.

So what is Version 2.0? In some ways it's the same book. The family history is here, and Berkeley, and Michigan, and the burn patients whose hands we opened, and the nurse I owed an apology, and my son's fourteen-centimeter tumor, which is still where this story begins. The crabs are still in the barrel, and the candle still becomes a fire. A few passages survive almost word for word, because they were true then and are true now. Keep a beginner's mind. Be kind. Think critically for yourself. I need those three sentences more than I did when I first typed them.

In other ways it's a different book. The first edition was written for doctors. This one is for anyone whose competence is good at hiding what it costs: founders, executives, physicians, lawyers, creators, the person other people lean on. My life is still a medical life and I haven't tried to scrub that out, but I no longer think the lessons belong to medicine. Medicine just gets to them early.

The thesis has changed. I used to say the problem was outside and the solution was inside. Now I think both places matter, and that the most dangerous idea in the first edition was the implication that if I was suffering, the fix was always my attitude. Sometimes the fix is a lawyer, or a psychiatrist, or a resignation letter, or eight hours of sleep. Agency, as I understand it now, isn't the belief that everything that happens to you is your fault. It's the belief that whatever has happened, your next move still belongs to you.

Money has moved. There is no real-estate teaching in this book, no acronym for financial freedom, no promise of a helicopter over the desert. Money is here as what it actually is: margin. It buys time and options and the ability to absorb a bad year. It doesn't buy meaning, and when I asked it to, it nearly cost me everything that did.

Mental health is handled with more care than I was capable of in 2021. Burnout is a real thing with a real definition, and it isn't depression, not mania, not a medication effect, and not a reason to stop looking. I've tried to be exact about what I know, what I suspect, and what I'll never be sure of, including about my own brain.

Material that no longer reflects my life or my views has been removed. I haven't replaced it with a tidier story. Where something is unknown, I say so. Where a fact needed a source, there is a note at the back rather than a claim in the middle of a paragraph.

Read it in order if you can. It's one argument, and the argument turns in the middle. If you're somewhere near the middle yourself, the last chapters were written for you, and the number you need in the United States is 988.

Patrick Tran
