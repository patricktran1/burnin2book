# The Achievement Bargain

Twelve years of school gets you a high school diploma. Four more gets you a bachelor's degree. For a physician you roughly double it: four years of medical school, then residency, then fellowship, until you emerge from what I've always called the twenty-fifth grade. I finished mine in my thirties. By then I'd been a student, in the technical sense of being evaluated by someone senior to me on a schedule I didn't set, for more than three-quarters of my life.

Nobody hands you a contract at the beginning of that, but there is one, and every ambitious person I know has signed a version of it. The terms are simple. You agree to postpone. Not just pleasure, though certainly that: the parties in college, the relationships you didn't have time for, the trips your friends took while you sat with a textbook. You postpone the whole business of living in your own life. In exchange, you're promised something that is never named out loud, because naming it would make it sound negotiable. What you're promised is safety.

Not money, though money is part of it. Safety. The permanent, structural certainty that you'll be all right, that you have earned a place that can't be taken, that the anxiety humming underneath everything will finally stop humming once the last credential is in hand. That is the deal. Study now, be safe later. And it works well enough, for long enough, that most of us never audit it.

I want to audit it here, because I signed it early and paid it in full and then found out what it does and doesn't buy.

* * *

The first thing worth noticing about a long deferral is what it does to your relationship with the present. If your twenties are an investment, then the days inside them aren't really days. They are units of a payment plan. You aren't at dinner; you're getting through dinner so you can get back to studying. You aren't on vacation; you're recovering so you can perform. A friend who spent a decade in litigation once told me that he couldn't remember any of his thirties, and I understood exactly what he meant. He remembered cases. The years those cases lived in had been converted into something else.

Medicine turns the conversion into a system. During residency you work eighty hours a week, you're paid very little, and someone else assigns your hours. When I was a resident in Washington I qualified for affordable housing. I was fortunate not to be carrying the debt many of my colleagues carried, and I still had no idea how any of this worked; I didn't get a credit card until after medical school, which kept me out of one trap and put me in another, since I spent years building no credit at all. Nobody teaches you money in medical school. Nobody teaches you how to run a practice, negotiate a contract, read an offer, or tell a good job from a bad one, and the culture makes it slightly shameful to ask. Professors don't discuss what they earn. Students whose parents are covering everything aren't encouraged to mention it. So an entire profession arrives at its first real paycheck with the financial literacy of a college sophomore and the spending latitude of a specialist.

The other thing the system teaches, more thoroughly than any subject, is that you don't own your time. Eight years of being told when to arrive isn't a neutral experience. It settles in. And then the training ends, and everyone congratulates you, and you take a job where you're still told when to arrive, only now the person telling you has a spreadsheet instead of a scalpel, and you've no framework for noticing that anything is wrong, because this is the only way you have ever lived.

Then comes the paycheck, and this is where the bargain gets interesting, because for about eighteen months it delivers. The money is real. After a decade of scarcity it feels enormous, and you buy some things you've wanted since you were nineteen, and it's genuinely wonderful. What nobody warns you about is that the bloom wears off, and it wears off on a schedule that has nothing to do with the amount. It wears off in the same number of months whether the number is two hundred thousand or eight hundred thousand. And when it goes, you're left standing in the life you spent twenty-five years buying, and you start noticing things: the paperwork attached to the simplest visit, the bureaucracy you don't understand, the administrator who has never done your job overruling your judgment about how to do it. You find yourself irritated with patients who don't listen, and then quietly appalled that you're irritated with patients, because helping patients is the thing you signed up for.

The measured version of this isn't encouraging. When researchers have tracked burnout among American physicians over the last decade and a half, at least one symptom has shown up in something like forty-five percent of them in 2023, thirty-eight percent in 2020, and nearly sixty-three percent in 2021, the worst year of the pandemic.[^c03-1] Whatever the deal promised, roughly half the profession isn't receiving it. And medicine isn't special here. It's simply a place where the terms are unusually visible, because we write them down and put them in a curriculum.

* * *

There is a grammar to the bargain, and once you hear it you can't stop hearing it. It goes: have, do, be. First I must *have* the thing, then I'll *do* what I want, and then I'll *be* happy. Once I have a million dollars, I'll take the trip, and then I'll be content. At ten million I'll retire, and then I'll be free. When the company reaches the valuation, or I make partner, or the practice is stable, or the book comes out, then I'll exhale.

The arithmetic is broken, and you can prove it without a study. Suppose you're making three hundred thousand dollars a year and you're unhappy. You won't be substantially happier at a million, or at ten million, because at every level you're still you, standing in the same body, waking up in the same head. Money changes your circumstances, and your circumstances matter, and I'll spend a whole chapter on exactly how much they matter. What money doesn't do is change the person doing the having.

I knew this in 2021. It's written down in the first edition: having, doing, and being were in the wrong order, and the sequence should run the other way. I meant every word of it, and I was in the middle of assembling a large collection of buildings at the time. This is the thing about the bargain: recognizing it intellectually isn't the same as being released from it. I could describe the trap in a chapter and then go straight back into it, because the description lived in one part of me and the wanting lived somewhere older.

The reason the wanting is older is that the bargain isn't really about money at all. It's about identity. When you've spent twenty-five years being the person who achieves, achievement stops being something you do and becomes the thing you are. That substitution is silent and almost impossible to notice from the inside, and it has one catastrophic property: if achievement is who you are, then failure isn't an event that happens to you. It's an announcement about your existence. A bad quarter isn't a bad quarter. It's evidence.

That is what makes the finish line move. If achievement is identity, you can never have enough of it, in the same way you can never be finished being yourself. So the milestone arrives, and you feel something for an hour or a week, and then the feeling fades and the target advances, and you tell yourself that the next one will be different. I've watched this in people I admire. A founder I know sold his company, the outcome he had been working toward for nine years, and was back at a desk building the next thing inside of a month. Not because he needed money. Because sitting still, with nothing being built, felt to him like disappearing.

There is also a renewal clause, and it's the cleverest part of the contract. The deal is never actually completed; it's refinanced at every stage, on terms you accept because you've already paid so much toward the old ones. Get into a good college, and the reward for getting in is a new agreement about medical school. Match into residency, and the reward is a longer agreement about fellowship. Finish training, and the reward is a partnership track, and then a leadership role, and then whatever comes after that. Each renewal is presented as the last one. Each is signed by a person who has invested too much to walk away, which is precisely the person you should never negotiate against.

The specifics change by industry and the structure doesn't. A senior associate bills two thousand hours a year for eight years on the understanding that partnership will convert hours into control, and discovers on arrival that partners bill too, and now have to bring in business as well. The founder who raises a round to buy himself freedom finds that he has acquired a board. Build an audience so that you'll never again have to make something you don't believe in, and you'll wake up to discover that the audience has preferences and that disappointing it costs money. In every case the mechanism is the same: the thing you traded the present for turns out to be an entrance rather than an exit.

There is a second job attached to all of this that nobody counts as work, which is the maintenance of the identity itself. Once being the person who achieves is what keeps you safe, you have to keep proving it, and proof has a shelf life. So you collect: another certification, another board seat, another line on the biography, another venture on the side. Each addition is defensible on its own terms and most of them are genuinely interesting, which is what makes the pattern so hard to see from inside. By the time I wrote the first edition I had accumulated an impressive number of these, and I printed the inventory in that book, in a passage I'll come back to later, because the way a man lists his commitments turns out to say more than the commitments do. If you had asked me why, I'd have said opportunity, and I'd have believed it. The honest answer is that the list was armor, and armor has to be added to, because the thing it protects never stops feeling exposed.

* * *

My family arrived at the bargain by a specific route, and I suspect a lot of readers arrived by the same one.

There is a Vietnamese proverb that says *không ai giàu ba họ, không ai khó ba đời*: no one stays rich for three generations, and no one stays poor for three. In the first edition I mangled the phrase and used it to talk about spoiled fourth-generation heirs crashing their fathers' cars. That isn't what it's for. What it describes is the arc of a family, and the middle of the arc is where people like me live.

The first generation does the crossing. They work in a way that leaves marks, and the work isn't for them; it's explicitly for the people who come after. The second generation grows up watching, and inherits an obligation that nobody has to state. You know, without being told, that the sacrifice must be justified. My mother learned biology and English simultaneously in a country she had just arrived in. My grandfather helped build a medical school on another continent. Against that, what exactly is a hard week? What is a complaint? The proverb's warning is about the generation that forgets. The version I lived was the opposite problem: a generation that couldn't forget for a moment, and that turned an inheritance of gratitude into a permanent debt.

That debt is a superb engine and a terrible governor. It will get you into medical school. It will also make it very difficult to notice when you have had enough, because "enough" is a concept that has no place in the arrangement. You can't repay a crossing. You can only keep producing evidence that it was worth it.

There is a spending version of this, too, and it's where the bargain finally becomes visible in a bank statement. After enough years of postponement, a person feels owed. Why shouldn't I have the nice car? I earned it, I waited longer than anyone should have to wait, and the car is a fraction of what I bring in. Telling you what to buy isn't my business; I've bought plenty, and the itemized version is coming. The trouble isn't the car. The trouble is that the car is being asked to settle an account, and it can't, so the next thing has to be bigger. Meanwhile your fixed costs have quietly risen to meet your income, and the job you thought you were choosing freely has become a job you can't leave. That is how a person with a large income ends up with no options, which is the exact opposite of what the twenty-five years were supposed to buy.

* * *

Every engine consumes something. It's worth asking what this one runs on.

![The rat found the water, and the water was in a hose it had to chew through.](../assets/figures/fig-11-rat-chewing-hose.png)

Not long after I moved into a new house, the refrigerator started acting up, and the handyman pulled it out from the wall and found the problem. A rat had gotten into the space behind it and chewed through the water line. Rats need water, and this was the nearest source, so it did what it was built to do and bit into the hose. What it couldn't know is that once air gets into the line, the water stops. The rat had solved a problem in a way that permanently removed its own supply. In the first edition I used this story to describe people who chase money without caring where it comes from, and it works fine for that. It works better as a description of me.

The achievement bargain runs on the achiever. Not on time, exactly, and not on effort in the abstract. It runs on sleep, on health, on attention, on the relationships that require unproductive hours, and on the part of your mind that would otherwise be doing nothing in particular. Those are the water line. And because none of them fail on the day you draw from them, the connection between the drawing and the failing is almost impossible to see. You take the sleep this week and pay for it in a decision you make eleven months from now. You take the attention from a person who loves you tonight and pay for it in a conversation you can't have four years from now, when you badly need to.

Mine failed in the most banal way possible. In January of 2020 I looked in the mirror and didn't see myself. I was the heaviest I had ever been. My jeans didn't fit, the seat belt sat tighter across me than it used to, and I'd gotten into the habit of undoing a notch on my belt after eating, which is a small thing you do quietly and don't mention. There had been a long run of nights that ended with fast food at an hour when nobody should be eating, because eating was the fastest available way to feel something good at the end of a day that had contained nothing good and no space. Nothing about that is dramatic. It's a completely ordinary way for a busy professional in his early thirties to arrive at a body he doesn't recognize, and it's what the bargain looks like when it starts drawing down the physical account. The mirror was the first statement I couldn't argue with, because it wasn't an opinion.

I want to say clearly that I did something about it, and that doing something about it worked, and that this is exactly the kind of story that makes people like me dangerous. I found the problem, applied effort, and the weight came off. Another data point for the machine. What I didn't do, and what didn't occur to me to do, was ask why my life had a shape in which the only available comfort at eleven at night was a drive-through. I fixed the readout. I never inspected the instrument.

* * *

None of this is an argument against ambition, and if the chapter has read that way I've written it badly. Deferring gratification is a real skill and a rare one. The capacity to work now for something that won't arrive for a decade is the reason surgeons exist, and companies, and symphonies, and the medical school in Cameroon. I wouldn't trade it, and I don't want you to trade it either.

What I'd change is the pricing. The bargain as most of us signed it has three defects, and none of them require you to stop being ambitious to fix.

The first is that the payment schedule is invisible. You are told what you'll get and never told what it costs, because the costs come out of accounts nobody itemizes. If somebody had shown me, at twenty-four, a projection of the sleep and the attention and the relationships this would draw down over fifteen years, I'd still have gone to medical school. I'd have gone differently.

The second is that the promised good is the wrong good. Safety isn't a thing achievement can deliver, because there is no achievement large enough to make a person unable to be hurt. I had that demonstrated to me twice, in ways I'll describe: once by a diagnosis in a child, and once by a set of documents. Nothing on my résumé was relevant to either.

The third defect is the one that nearly killed me. When your identity is fused to your output, you lose the ability to distinguish a bad outcome from a verdict on your existence. And a person who can't make that distinction, under sufficient pressure, will do arithmetic that a person who can make it would never do.

If you want a place to start, and this is the only practical thing I'll offer in this chapter, it's to write down what enough looks like while you're calm. Not a number by itself; a number attached to a life. How much margin, in dollars and in hours, would let you turn down work you don't want. What you won't miss: which nights, which weeks, whose birthdays. What you'd need to be true to say that this stage of the deal is complete, so that when you arrive you can tell that you have arrived. Doing it in advance matters, because the definition is written by the calm version of you and consulted by the one under load, and the one under load will happily revise it upward every time. I never wrote mine down. When the pressure came, I'd no way to tell the difference between losing money and losing myself, because I'd never specified which one I was.

Before any of that, though, there is a nearer question, which is why someone as analytical as I am could carry a broken contract for two decades without ever reading it. I have an answer. It's the same faculty that made me good at all of this in the first place, and it comes with a blind spot in the shape of my own face.

* * *

Read the contract you signed before you were old enough to read it. Then decide, on purpose and in daylight, which of its terms you still agree to.
