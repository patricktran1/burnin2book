# What I Want My Children to Know

I have written thousands of notes for readers I would never meet. A chart note is addressed to whoever picks up the chart next: the covering physician at three in the morning, the surgeon who inherits the patient a decade from now, the nurse who needs to know what I was thinking when I wrote the order. You write those notes plainly, because the reader is in a hurry and you will not be in the room to explain yourself. This chapter is that kind of note. It is addressed to you, Adrian, and to any child of mine who reads this later. I do not know how old you will be when you get to it, or what kind of man I will have become by then, so I am going to put down what I know now and try not to explain more than the reader needs.

The first edition of this book had a great deal in it about you and almost nothing in it for you. It was written to an audience, and you were the material: the fourteen centimeters, the port, the fevers, the boy still small enough to carry. I do not regret writing about your illness, and I have kept most of it, because it happened and because it changed the shape of my life. But a chapter with your name in it should be different from a chapter that uses your name, and it took me until this edition to see the difference.

* * *

The truest scene I put in the first book was not the one I thought was true at the time. It was bedtime. You loved the fish, and you liked to help feed them, so you put your fingers in the fish food and sprinkled it into the aquarium. Then I would try to brush your teeth, and you would fight me, not because you were two but because the chemotherapy made you nauseated, and a toothbrush in the mouth of a nauseated person is an act of aggression. Then a book. Then the soft weighted Mickey Mouse a friend had brought to the hospital, which you cuddled along with a blanket, and the night-light, and the music. Then a kiss, and the door.

I put that routine in a chapter about how the work continues, and I used it to illustrate a saying. The saying was the least important thing on the page. What I was actually recording, without knowing it, was the one part of that year that was not a fight. Everything else about your treatment was a schedule and a war: blood counts, cycles, the feeding tube in your nose, the thick curly hair that came out, the fever that meant I packed the bag and drove back to the hospital. Bedtime was the ten minutes when nothing was being done to you. Fish, teeth, book, Mickey, light, music, kiss. Nobody was measuring anything.

I want you to know that I was neither a saint nor a wreck in those months. I went to work. I put on a face for my patients, and in the first edition I was proud of that, and elsewhere in this one I take the pride apart. At bedtime the face came off, because you did not need it. You needed the fish fed. There is a kind of love that consists mostly of showing up for the same ten minutes in the same order every night, and I learned it from you, before you could say the word for it.

* * *

The radiation is the part I still turn over. As it was explained to me, when a tumor like yours has come out, the radiation that follows crosses the spine, and if you treat only one side of a growing child's spine, the child grows crooked. So they treated straight across the middle, evenly, which protects you from scoliosis and costs you something else: an inch or two of the height you would otherwise have had. I understood the trade-off as a physician. Then I went home and thought, as a father, I don't want my child to be short. I wrote that sentence down in 2021 and I am leaving it in, because it is exactly what a man thinks when he still believes the future is a contest.

I am not a tall man. Patients have told me for years that I look twelve, which is a compliment right up until you are about to remove a cancer from their nose. So the inch had a personal edge. But it was also the achievement machine talking in a father's voice, and I could hear it. I wanted you to have everything and then some, and an inch was on the list, and so, for a while, was the worry about whether chemotherapy would leave you able to have children of your own. Those were the two futures I grieved in advance, height and grandchildren, while you slept in the next room with a port under your skin.

Then I would walk in and watch you breathe, and the list went blank. I wrote in the first edition, "What are any of these things compared to his precious life, his chance to just breathe and live on earth?" I was right, and being right did not stick. The list came back. It comes back even now, every few weeks, in some new form, and I have to do the sorting again. What the inch taught me is not that small worries are foolish. It is that I had spent my life sorting worries by how much they threatened my standing and almost never by how much they threatened my life, that a tumor sorted them correctly for me in a single afternoon, and that the correct order does not hold on its own. You have to redo it. I redo it by looking at you.

* * *

You inherited some things from me that I did not choose to give you. One of them may be in your heart. During the year of your treatment, your grandfather fainted, went to an emergency room, and came out with a diagnosis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, a thickening of one wall of the heart that is written into the genes rather than caused by anything a person does. Chapter 1 tells that story and gives the odds. Here I will only say that if his disease comes from one of the known variants, there is a real chance I carry it, and if I do, a real chance you do. Whether either of us does is a question for a laboratory and a cardiologist, and this book is not where I am going to answer it. What I can tell you is what the diagnosis looks like from inside a family: a man who now has a name for something, a plan for it, and appointments he keeps. The textbook presentation of this disease is a healthy young athlete collapsing on a court. Its usual reality, once it has a name, is medicine and follow-up. Knowing beats not knowing, and that is the whole of my cardiology advice.

The other inheritance is a temperament, and I will be surprised if you have escaped it. I lean forward. Give me a problem and I start working and do not stop, and for most of my life that was the best thing about me and the thing everyone praised. It carried me through Berkeley and Michigan and a residency I hated and a fellowship I loved. It got you diagnosed, because when one side of your belly looked different from the other I did not wait to see whether it would resolve. I want you to have that. I would not trade it out of you if I could.

But I want you to know its failure mode, which nobody told me, or told me in a way I could hear. The trait that will not stop working on a problem will not stop working on you. When the trouble is inside your own head, leaning forward does not help; it takes the fear and makes a project of it, and the project runs all night. In the years this book is mostly about I learned that my own mind has tendencies I did not know it had, that some of them probably run deeper than mood, and that a very capable person can reason his way past every warning sign, because capability is the warning sign's best disguise. Whether any of that is in your wiring I do not know, and no one has told me it is.

So here is what I want you to do with the temperament. Use it on the world. Do not use it on yourself in the dark. Treat your mind the way your grandfather treats his heart: as an organ that came with a family history, that deserves its own specialist, and that is allowed to need medicine without anyone in this family reading the medicine as a verdict. If you ever need a pill for your mood, take it with exactly as much drama as a beta blocker deserves, which is none.

* * *

Your grandmother came to this country from Vietnam at seventeen, in the middle of a war, and learned English and biology in the same semesters so that she could become a physician. Families like ours run on a logic I obeyed for thirty years without examining it: the first generation sacrifices, the second generation's job is to make the sacrifice worth it, and the way you make it worth it is with results. Results meant medical school, and summa cum laude, and a title with the word surgeon in it. I do not want to mock that logic. It worked, it was made out of love, and the people who ran it on me had crossed an ocean to give me the chance to be run on.

But I am releasing you from it. You do not owe me a return on investment. Your grandparents gave me opportunity, I have tried to give you opportunity, and opportunity is a door rather than a debt: a door you never walk through is still a door I was glad to hold open. If you become a physician I will be happy. If you become a kindergarten teacher or a pianist, like your aunt and your uncle, I will be happy in exactly the same amount, and I have practiced saying so until I believe it.

* * *

You will read, earlier in this book, about the years when I believed I had failed catastrophically. I am not going to tell that story again, and parts of it I will never tell, because they are not only mine. What I want you to know is what I did with the failure, which was the wrong thing. I hid it. I got up before dawn for the calls, went to work, operated well, and let the people who loved me believe that a man operating well was a man who was fine. A failed deal is not a failed person; Chapter 7 makes that argument and I will not repeat it. What I will add is the part that concerns you. The hiding was worse than the failure, and it was the hiding, not the failure, that nearly cost you a father. If you ever fail at something large, and if you are my child you will attempt things large enough to fail at, I want you to fail out loud, in the kitchen, early, while it is still a thing that happened and not yet a thing you are.

Money, in one paragraph, because I once wrote a book that was partly about it and I owe you the shorter version. Money bought us time when you were sick. I could take unscheduled weeks off and sit in a hospital room, and I never had to choose between your treatment and the bills, and I will not pretend that was nothing; it was one of the mercies of that year, and plenty of families do not get it. Later, the fear of losing money was one of the things that took me apart, which tells you it had stopped being a tool and become the place where I kept my sense of being a worthwhile person. So keep enough that a bad year does not become a catastrophe, and do not keep it where you keep your worth. That is the whole of my financial advice, and it is not advice so much as a confession with a suggestion attached.

* * *

There is a sentence I could not say for a long time. Small children say it a hundred times a day, about shoes and cups and the top of the slide, without a trace of shame, and somewhere between your age then and my age now I had unlearned it. The sentence is I need help. In the profession I trained for it is the sentence you are least rewarded for saying, and in the version of myself I had built it was structurally impossible, because the whole structure was a man who did not need it.

In my clinic the patients who do best are the ones who come in early. A skin cancer caught when it is the size of a grain of rice is a short morning and a small line you will not be able to find a year later. The same cancer, ignored for three years because the person was busy or brave or embarrassed, is a different kind of afternoon, and I have spent a large part of my working life in those afternoons, rebuilding what waiting took. Nobody is ashamed of a small spot. People are ashamed of the big one, because it is evidence that they waited, and the shame makes them wait longer. I am telling you this because it is exactly how the mind works too, and because I knew it about skin for my whole career without once applying it to myself.

So say it early, and say it badly if you have to. The right time is before you are sure it is warranted, when the trouble is still small and a little embarrassing and you suspect you are overreacting. Tell a person, not a page. And know that the people who love you would rather hear it a hundred times about nothing than once, too late, about something.

* * *

I changed direction once in a way that alarmed everyone, including me. I had matched into a residency I had spent years competing to get into, and partway through I told a department chair that I did not think I was happy in plastic surgery and that I was supposed to be a dermatologist. Chapter 2 tells that story. What I want you to take from it is only this: I quit something I had organized a decade around, and the sky held. I walked away from a résumé and toward a life, and the résumé turned out to be the smaller thing.

The second change of direction was harder and has no specialty attached. Some years later I had to leave a version of myself: the one who could not be helped, who mistook being needed for being well, who would rather have vanished than been seen not coping. Leaving him was slower than leaving surgery and much less cinematic, with no match day at the end. I mention it because you may someday build a version of yourself that you then have to leave, and I want you to know from someone who has done it that leaving is allowed, and that the people who love you are not attached to the version. They are attached to you.

* * *

In the first edition, a few pages after the fish and the toothbrush, I wrote this: "My favorite definition of Hell is: when you die, you meet the version of you that you could have been." You look at him, I explained, and you see the gap between him and the real you, and you understand that you wasted your life. I wrote that I did not want that for myself, or for my son, or for anyone.

I retract it. Of every sentence in that book, this is the one I most want you never to believe. It is the achievement trap in a robe. It takes the machine that had run me since kindergarten, the one that measures and compares and finds the gap, and promotes it to the afterlife, so that even dying becomes a performance review. The man who wrote that sentence believed he was describing a spiritual truth. He was describing his own anxiety and giving it a title. He had not yet met the version of himself he was about to become, and he would not have recognized him, and he would have called him the failure.

Here is what I believe instead. The version of you that you could have been is not waiting anywhere. There is no tape measure at the end. There is the person you are, in the life you have, on the day you have it, and the only comparison worth making is between that person and the one he was yesterday, and even that one should be made gently and not too often. I spent decades measuring myself against a ghost with better numbers. The radiation took an inch or two from you, and nobody is going to measure it but me, and I have stopped.

I will keep the garden, though. Right after the business about Hell, I wrote about a garden that is never finished: leaves fall, plants die, you work for an hour and it is better and not perfect, and the next day you do it again. I also wrote that you then rest in the hot tub, counting your roses and your avocado trees, which tells you something about the man who wrote it that he did not know he was telling you. But the garden was right. The work repeats. That is not a punishment and not a sign that you are behind. It is what tending anything looks like, including a child, and including a mind.

* * *

I also wrote, in that chapter, that I could not wait for the day you were in middle school and arguing with me because you would rather play video games than do your homework, and I predicted that by then video games would probably be homework. I stand by the prediction and by the wanting. I want the ordinary fights. I want to be the tedious father who says homework is good for you and is wrong about half the time. But I also wrote that one day, grown, you would call me and say, "Thank you, Dad, for forcing me to do my science homework." You do not have to make that call. The call I want is a different one.

Here it is, and it is the sentence this chapter exists to carry. I would rather have you here. I would rather have you here than have you be anything in particular: here having quit medical school in the second year, here having failed at the business, here having disappointed me in some way I cannot yet imagine and will survive. I would rather have you here at four in the morning, on the phone, because you cannot sleep and the story in your head has started to sound like a fact. I have been awake at four in the morning for far worse reasons than a call from you. Quit anything. Fail at anything. What you may not do is decide, alone, in the dark, that the people who love you would be better off, because I know what the dark does with a decision like that, and I know that it lies.

* * *

Then it was morning. I am usually up before you, and for a while the house is mine, and then it is not, which is the better part. The mornings after those bedtimes were nothing in particular, and that is what I remember about them and what I want you to know. The fish needed feeding again. The toothbrush was still an act of aggression. You ran your Hot Wheels across my legs as if I were a road, which I wrote about in the first edition and which remains the best use anyone has found for me. You talked to the trees on the way to the car. Then I went to work and took cancers off the faces of people old enough to be your great-grandparents, and you went off to do whatever you did, and nothing about any of it was a lesson. It was morning, and I would like as many of them as I can get.
