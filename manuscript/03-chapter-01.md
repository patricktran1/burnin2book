# Who Am I, and How Did I Get Here?

Every fall, high school seniors sit for the portraits that will hang in their parents' hallways for the next thirty years, and then they submit a quote to be printed under their name in the yearbook. The quote is supposed to sum you up: a lyric, a line from a movie, an inside joke that three friends will understand. Most people agonize over it. I didn't. Under my smiling seventeen-year-old face, months before a single college admissions letter would reach anyone's mailbox, I had them print "Berkeley, Class of 2010."

I thought I was going to Berkeley. I knew I was going to Berkeley. When I tell this story now, people ask what would have happened if I hadn't gotten in, and the honest answer is that the question never occurred to me. Not once, not for a moment. I hadn't weighed the possibility of rejection and decided the odds were in my favor; the possibility simply didn't exist in my head. A yearbook quote is supposed to be a summary of who you've been. Mine was a prediction, printed in advance, by someone who didn't distinguish between the two.

I did get in. That matters to the story, because if I hadn't, the yearbook would be a funny anecdote about a cocky kid and this would be a different book. Instead, the yearbook became the first document in a long file of evidence that my confidence and reality tended to agree with each other. For most of my life, they did. Much of this book is about what happens when a person built on that agreement finally meets a situation where the two come apart. Before that, though, you should know where the confidence came from. It didn't come from nowhere.

* * *

It would be easy to say I was destined for medicine. The truth is duller and more interesting: I was good at it, it was around, and being good at things was how I knew I was safe.

Even so, "it was around" understates the case. My mother came to the United States from Vietnam during the war. At seventeen, roughly the age I was when I ordered my yearbook prophecy, she was probably not spending much energy on senior quotes. She enrolled in college in Long Beach with two jobs in front of her: learn the science she would need to get into medical school, and learn English. She did both at once, and by the end of her undergraduate years she was fluent in biology and in the language of the country she now lived in. Then she moved north to start medical school at the University of California, San Francisco, where she met my father. That is where I come into the story, at least in principle.

My father was also a Vietnamese immigrant, and also at UCSF, training as a resident in prosthodontics, the branch of dentistry that rebuilds what a mouth has lost. His father, my grandfather, was an obstetrician and gynecologist who had trained at Johns Hopkins. My grandfather's original plan was to learn medicine in America and bring it home, and for a while that is exactly what he did. He served the government of South Vietnam as its minister of health, something like a surgeon general, helping to establish hospitals and clinics. Later he went to work for the World Health Organization, and the whole family packed up and moved to Cameroon, which must have felt to my father and his siblings like the opening chapter of an adventure novel. Cameroon at the time was losing its best students to universities in Europe and the United States, and the students weren't coming back. My grandfather's assignment was to help build a medical school so that the country could train its own doctors and give them a reason to stay.

So when I say medicine was around, I mean that it was the family business in the most literal sense, and that it came with a story about what a doctor is for. A doctor is someone who builds the hospital where there was no hospital. A doctor is someone who leaves a country and a language behind and learns a new one while also learning organic chemistry. The people in that story did hard things because the hard things were in front of them and nobody else was going to do them. I absorbed that before I could have put it into words. What I absorbed along with it, without anyone intending to teach it, was that the way you earn your place in a family like that is by being very, very good.

By the time I arrived, sandwiched between an older sister and a younger brother, my parents had settled in Piedmont, a small city in the East Bay, tucked in near Oakland and not far from the Berkeley campus. My mother became an allergist and immunologist. She also bought a handful of rental houses in California, whose tenants come back into this story later. Both my parents speak Vietnamese, English, and French, and they wanted us to know where they had come from even as they were still figuring out where they had landed, down to the small cultural mysteries nobody warns you about, like how to stand in a line the American way. An ocean separated them from everything they had grown up with. They didn't talk about that much. They worked.

I did well in school from the beginning, with one early exception that I still enjoy. At St. Leo's, where I went to kindergarten, the teachers sent each child home every day with a paper apple colored to reflect that day's behavior. Red was good. Green was a little less good, then yellow, and then there was brown, which was my specialty. I came home with brown apples on a more or less daily basis. As far as I can reconstruct it, I wasn't a bad kid; I was a kid with a great deal of forward motion and not much interest in where the lines were. The science came easily, and biology especially, and by high school it felt like I was on a straight-shot path to having "Dr." in front of my name. The brown apples were the first evidence that the engine and the steering were two separate systems. Nobody, including me, thought of it that way at the time.

* * *

Here is a detail that took me a long time to find interesting. My extended family contains enough physicians to staff a small hospital. Among my aunts and uncles there is a radiologist, a family medicine doctor, two orthopedic surgeons, an ear, nose, and throat surgeon, a urologist, an ophthalmologist, an internist, an obstetrician-gynecologist, a nuclear medicine physician, a cardiologist, a radiation oncologist, and an oral and maxillofacial surgeon, plus my mother the allergist and my father the prosthodontist. Between the twenty-one cousins on my mother's side and the fifteen on my father's, that makes thirty-six of us in my generation who grew up eating dinner with those people. Guess how many went into medicine.

If you guessed more than three, you'd be wrong. One cousin on my mother's side became a doctor, and another had just gotten into medical school when I wrote the first edition of this book. On my father's side, I'm it. My sister Natalie teaches kindergarten. My brother Eric earned a Doctor of Musical Arts and plays the piano beautifully, which made him another Dr. Tran, just not the kind my mother had hoped for.

In the first edition I asked how I came to be the only one who got the memo, and then I corrected myself: the better question was how I came to be the only one who didn't. My cousins and siblings and I all had the same up-close look at what a doctor's life involved. We saw the pagers at dinner and the fatigue and the phone calls that couldn't wait. We also saw something subtler, which is that people who appeared to be in positions of power were often at the mercy of forces far beyond their control: call schedules, administrators, insurance companies, and more. Lions and tigers and bears. My cousins looked at all that and quietly made other plans. I looked at the same evidence and saw a job I could be excellent at, and being excellent at something had always been the end of the analysis. Thirty-five reasonable people reached one conclusion from the same facts, and I reached the other. I don't think I was braver than they were. I think I was more certain, and at the time I couldn't have told you the difference.

* * *

Whatever the "doctor gene" is, I clearly got it. It turns out it may not have been the only thing handed down to me, and I want to tell that part here, even though it happened much later, because it belongs to the question of what a person is made of.

During the year Adrian was being treated for cancer, my father passed out. He was rushed to an emergency room, where the immediate worry was a heart attack; his electrocardiogram showed concerning signs, and he went to the catheterization lab so the cardiologists could look for a blocked artery. There was no significant blockage. That was the good news. The echocardiogram that followed showed something else: my father has hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. One wall of his heart is about two and a half times thicker than it should be, close to an inch of muscle where there ought to be about ten millimeters. This wasn't the result of a poor diet or stress or anything he did. It's genetic.

The mechanics of what happened to him are worth understanding, because they show how a condition can stay invisible for most of a lifetime and then announce itself in an afternoon. My father had a stomach bug and became dehydrated, which meant less blood was returning to fill his heart. With the chamber underfilled, the thickened wall got in the way when the left ventricle squeezed, and less blood made it out to the body and up to the brain. He lost consciousness. In medical school we learned this as HOCM, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, and the classic teaching case is a seemingly healthy twenty-year-old who collapses on a basketball court and dies without warning, with nobody having had any idea that something was wrong. That could easily have been my father, at any point in his life. And if his disease comes from one of the known pathogenic variants, each of his first-degree relatives has a fifty percent chance of carrying it too.[^c01-1] That includes me. That includes Adrian.

The condition is manageable once you know about it, which is the whole point. Many people do well on nothing more than a beta blocker, a single daily pill. There are operations to resect the thickened wall and correct the mechanical problem. There is a technique called alcohol septal ablation, in which a cardiologist threads a catheter into the heart and induces a small, controlled heart attack in the muscle that is causing the obstruction, so that the wall thins. And there are implantable defibrillators, devices placed under the skin that shock the heart back into rhythm if it slips into one of the dangerous arrhythmias that are a main cause of death in this disease, doing the same job a code team does with paddles, except that the device goes home with you. With the right treatment and some luck, my father may live for decades. I'd need to be tested, and so would Adrian.

When I told friends about my father, on top of everything happening with my son, they said the things kind people say: what a year this has been for you and your family, we're so sorry you're dealing with all of this. In the first edition of this book I printed my response on its own line. It read, "How lucky am I?" And I meant it. My father was alive. He had passed out, gone to the hospital, and received a diagnosis within days; I had physician friends who made themselves available instantly to answer questions and make referrals; I now knew about a risk to myself and my son that I could actually do something about. I thought of all the times I'd been out of breath, overheated from a hard workout, or wired from partying like there was no tomorrow, and I marveled that I was still here. Every one of those thoughts was true.

What I notice now, reading that passage, is the speed. My father nearly died, I learned that I might carry the same thing, and within a paragraph I had converted the whole event into gratitude. I was proud of that at the time; I thought it was the result of years of reading and coaching and work on myself, and partly it was. It was also a reflex, and reflexes don't evaluate the situation before they fire. Mine could take any piece of bad news and metabolize it into a reason to feel fortunate before I had actually sat with what the news meant. For most of my life that was a strength, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. There would come a time, not many years after I typed "How lucky am I?", when the reflex would meet something it couldn't process, and I'd find out what happens to a person whose main coping mechanism fails silently. I'll tell that story when we get to it. For now I only want to mark the reflex as one of the things handed down to me, or built into me, alongside the doctor gene and whatever my father's heart may have given me.

* * *

I stayed close to home for college. Berkeley was a short drive away, and I had, after all, already announced it. I spent my freshman year in the dorms and concluded that while dorm life might be the center of somebody's college experience, it wasn't going to be the center of mine, so I moved back into my parents' house and rented a parking spot on campus for a fraction of what four years of housing would have cost. My parents helped pay my tuition. I was aware even then how fortunate that made me, and I'm more aware of it now; a great deal of what I'd later call discipline was made possible by not having to worry about rent.

Berkeley was also the first place the engine met real resistance. My freshman year I took Chem 1A, which is what people call a weeder course: about fifteen hundred of us started it, and the expectation was that more than half wouldn't continue in the sciences. Nobody was holding anyone's hand. I'd come from very good schools with small classes and teachers who knew my name and my brown-apple history, and now I was one of fifteen hundred in a lecture hall, and the lecture hall didn't care whether I'd been the smartest kid in Piedmont. I found, to my relief, that when I worked, the system responded. That is a dangerous thing to learn at eighteen, because it's true in a chemistry course and it isn't universally true in life, and the course doesn't tell you which lessons transfer.

By my sophomore year I was teaching a chemistry lab to freshmen, and I did a thing that I still find funny and slightly embarrassing. I was doing research in a biology lab, which was true, so I told my students that I was a researcher, which was also true, and I let them assume I was a graduate student, which wasn't. I didn't tell them I was an undergraduate because I didn't want them to take me less seriously. There I was at nineteen, constructing a professional backstory so that people would believe I belonged at the front of the room. If you want a small, harmless example of how early I learned to manage other people's perception of my competence, there it is. It worked, incidentally. The students never found out, the lab went well, and I graduated summa cum laude in molecular and cell biology with the sense that the world was, as I put it then, my oyster.

* * *

The University of Michigan Medical School offered me a merit scholarship, and I took it. I'd been at the top of my class in high school, and at Berkeley, which isn't an easy place, I'd done well enough to be near the top again. If I may put it bluntly, I was hot shit, and I knew it.

At Michigan, everyone seemed like hot shit. It took me an embarrassingly long time to work out why: every one of my classmates had been at the top of a class somewhere, and most of us had spent our lives being the smartest person in whatever room we happened to be in. Now the room was full of us. This was the first real correction my confidence ever received, and I absorbed it the way I absorbed everything, by settling in and buckling down. Ordinary wasn't the conclusion I drew. What I concluded was that I needed to work harder, which had always been the answer before and which was, in this case, the right answer as well. That is the trouble with a strategy that works. It keeps being confirmed until the day it isn't.

I didn't know what kind of doctor I wanted to be. I'd watched my parents, so I had some idea what the life looked like, but I kept my eyes open and waited for a specialty to declare itself. It did, during a plastic surgery rotation, and it did so in a single hour.

The patient was a man who had been burned in a kerosene fire. The scars on his hand had contracted as they healed, as burn scars do, pulling the skin tight across the palm and fingers until he could no longer fully close his hand. Think about how much of a day requires a closed hand: a doorknob, a fork, a steering wheel, a child's shoulder. He had been living without that. The surgeon was Paul Cederna, and the operation was a series of Z-plasties, a technique for cutting a tight scar into interlocking triangles of skin and swapping their positions so that the line of tension is lengthened and turned in a new direction. It's geometry. Each Z-plasty releases a little more of the contracture. And with the completion of each one, Dr. Cederna would breathe out a long sigh, as if he were feeling the release of the scar's tension in his own body: "Ahhhhh."

I'd never seen anything like it. The patient went to sleep with a hand he couldn't use, and an hour later he woke up with a hand he could. Nothing had been transplanted and no miracle drug had been given; the same tissue had simply been rearranged by someone who understood exactly where to cut. I stood there and thought, *that*. I want to do that.

I'll spoil it now: I don't practice burn reconstruction today, and that I didn't stay in plastic surgery at all. But I want to be fair to the young man in that operating room, because he was right about something. What drew me toward medicine wasn't only that I was good at it. There was real power in the room that day, the kind that gives a person back the ordinary tools of his life, and I recognized it and wanted to be the one holding it. Wanting that isn't a character flaw. It's one of the better things about me. The problem was never the wanting; it was everything I had bolted onto it without noticing.

* * *

By the time I left Michigan I had a fully assembled way of dealing with the world, and it's worth describing plainly, because the rest of this book is about what it cost. A problem, to me, was an invitation to lean in. I didn't experience difficulty as a reason to step back and ask whether the difficulty was telling me something; I experienced it as a signal to build a schedule, and the schedule as a promise that the difficulty would yield. A syllabus with a thousand pages on it wasn't intimidating. It was arithmetic: pages divided by days, with a margin built in for the week I might get sick. Discomfort of any kind became a signal to work, work reliably made the discomfort go away, and so I never had to develop any other tool for discomfort. Why would I? The one I had was excellent.

There is a version of this description that sounds like a compliment, and for a long time I took it as one. Medicine took it as one too. Every stage of my training rewarded exactly this, and every reward confirmed that the machine was correct. The yearbook, the weeder course, the lab I taught under a slightly inflated résumé, the scholarship, the man whose hand opened: each was a data point, and every point sat higher than the last. What the points couldn't show me was that I'd never actually tested the machine on a problem that didn't respond to effort. I'd only ever met the other kind.

So that is who was standing there at the start of surgical training. A young man from a family of doctors, who had learned early that being very good was how you earned your place; who had printed his future in a yearbook before the evidence arrived and been proven right; who had been humbled once, briefly, in Ann Arbor, and had converted the humbling into a study schedule; who had watched a hand open and decided that was what he was for. He had never failed at anything that mattered. He believed, without ever having to say it out loud, that there was no problem in the world he couldn't outwork. That was the person who showed up for the first day of surgical residency.

* * *

Your ambition isn't the problem, and you don't owe anyone an apology for wanting what you want. Go and find out what you bolted onto it while you weren't looking.
