# Where Am I Going?

Medical school is a volume problem before it is anything else. The material itself is not conceptually harder than upper-level college biology; biochemistry is biochemistry, and genetics is genetics. What changes is the rate. Content that a university would spread across a semester arrives in a week, and then another week's worth arrives on top of it, and the week after that does not wait for you to finish the first two. There is a specific kind of panic that sets in around the third month, when you understand that you will never again feel caught up, and that the feeling of being caught up was something you were going to have to learn to live without.

That part I was built for. Give me a firehose and a calendar and I will find a way to drink. My first two preclinical years were, if I am honest, the best-designed life I have ever had. The lectures were recorded, so I did not attend them; I watched at double speed at home, whenever I felt like it. Meals happened when I was hungry. Martial arts or the gym happened twice a day. There was an enormous amount to learn and I was in control of every hour I spent learning it, and I mistook that combination for a permanent feature of the profession rather than a two-year accident of curriculum design.

What I was not built for was the part where the material stops being the point.

The first sign came during a pediatrics rotation, and I was the one who caused it. I had been reading heavily, the way I always read, and a friend of mine, another medical student, was presenting a patient. Then I spoke up. What I said is gone from my memory; what stayed is that it was correct and that it was not mine to say. The residents did not have that piece of information at that moment, and I put it in the room, in front of them, because having information and displaying it had always been the same action in my mind. My friend was furious. The residents, who had been up all night and were being quietly corrected by a student, were something worse than furious; they were embarrassed. I had not set out to hurt anyone. That is the part I would ask you to sit with, because it is the part that took me years to understand. I was insecure and demonstrating my knowledge, and the demonstration cost other people something, and I did not see the cost until it was on their faces.

Nobody had told me the rules had changed. For twenty years my life had been tests, grades, and projects, absorbing more material than the person next to me and then proving it. That is not a trivial skill, and I do not want to be falsely modest about it; knowledge is a huge part of being good at this. But a clinical team is not an exam. You are not competing with your classmates anymore; you are working with a team, and the currency is not what you know but whether you make the work lighter for the people around you. You need communication and bedside manner and a dozen soft skills that do not develop while you are studying fourteen hours a day. It is not enough to be the smartest guy in the room. You have to be the most useful one, which frequently means being the one who does the scut work without complaining so that your resident, who is drowning, can breathe.

I did not get it. Science came naturally; this did not. And it truly is a game you are playing on rotations, with rules that nobody prints anywhere. A medical student is not, in strict terms, useful to a patient. Your scope is too narrow and your license does not exist yet. What you can do is help the team, and if you help the team, your resident says something good to the attending, and the attending writes something good about you, and that is how you move. Once I understood the mechanics I could play the game, and I played it well. It bothered me for years that it was a game at all.

The other thing rotations took from me was my schedule, which turned out to be more load-bearing than I knew. There is no double speed on a surgery rotation. You are there at four in the morning to pre-round, in the dark, every day, and the day ends when it ends. You do not control your time or your space or the order in which things happen to you. In the language people use about training, you are being broken down so you can be built back up as a doctor. I understood the theory. Living inside it, after two years of designing my own days, was a different experience.

Here is the moment I always come back to from that year. The first real task a medical student gets in a surgical case is to cut a suture. That is it. The surgeon ties the knot, and you take the scissors and cut the tails at the right length. They hand you the scissors, and your hands are shaking, because the surgeon is a large personality who is fully prepared to come down on you if the tails are too long or too short, and the room is quiet, and everyone is looking. Cutting is a skill I mastered in kindergarten, in the same building where I was collecting brown apples for behavior. And there I stood, in the equivalent of the twentieth grade, hands sweating, wondering whether a scissor cut on a piece of suture was about to determine the trajectory of my career.

It is absurd when you write it down. It was not absurd at the time, and the fact that it was not absurd is the actual finding. A training culture had convinced a group of very capable adults that a two-millimeter difference in a suture tail was an occasion for terror. That culture was so normal to everyone inside it that nobody thought to name it, which is roughly the definition of a culture.

* * *

Surgeons quiz students in public, and the practice has a name I have never liked: pimping. It works like this. Four students are standing at the table, and the attending, mid-case, asks who can tell him what exits the inferior vena cava at a given level, or which renal artery comes off the aorta first, the left or the right. Somebody answers. Then it is the common causes of pancreatitis, and someone gets to scorpion sting, and the attending says, "Oh, great job. Which species of scorpion?" Some of it is legitimately clinically relevant anatomy that you should be able to produce under pressure, because one day you will be producing it under more pressure than this. Some of it is not. There were days I was asked why flamingos are pink, or which musician was responsible for the terrible song the attending insisted on playing during major cases. The questions were a test of composure as much as knowledge, and there is an argument for that. There is also a race in the room to answer first, so the attending knows which of the four deserves to be there, and after a while you notice that the race is the point.

I could answer. That was not my problem. My problem was cultural, and it took me a long time to see it as a cultural problem rather than a personal defect. I come from an Asian family, where the operating principle is that children are seen and not heard, and where being quiet and reserved and not making a scene is a form of respect. Western medicine values the opposite. It rewards the person who speaks up and sounds certain. If you are quiet, people conclude that you do not know, or are not paying attention, or are not a team player, and if you were raised to believe that shouting out an answer is rude, you will not seem smart even on the days when you have the answer. I was shy, and it took years to find my voice, and finding it required deciding that not rocking the boat, which had been a virtue in my house, was going to cost me my career.

I learned to speak up. Then, a decade later, I would learn what it is like to be a person who knows exactly how to project confidence in a room and cannot say a true sentence about his own state to anyone. Those two skills are more closely related than they look.

* * *

I matched at Indiana University in integrated plastic and reconstructive surgery, one of the more competitive paths in medicine, six years long. That was the plan: six years there, and then a life spent doing burn reconstruction, the thing that had grabbed me in Ann Arbor.

The reality of burn surgery is that most of it is not reconstruction. It is acute care. When somebody comes in burned, the operations that keep them alive are skin grafting, critical care, and fluid management, and they are nothing like the elegant hour I had watched Paul Cederna spend on a contracted hand. They are massive grafts in a hot operating room, hours long, with a team of people around you: the anesthesiologist, the residents, the scrub techs, the perioperative nurses, and then the ICU team who take over afterward. I could see that even if I eventually became captain of that ship, I would not have much control over the sailing. What I would have was responsibility, which is not the same thing.

Picture a twenty-year-old kid burned in a car fire. He is going to survive the first week, probably, and I am the one sitting with his family explaining what surviving would mean: constant pain, an inability to walk or eat or move or breathe on his own, a life he would not recognize and might not want. That conversation is part of the specialty. Somebody has to be able to have it, and the people who can are worth their weight in anything you care to name.

I was miserable.

I want to be careful here, because "miserable" is a word people use about a bad quarter. This was not that. I was in the sixth or seventh year of a life I had designed entirely around a single destination, I had arrived at the destination, and the destination was wrong. There was no version of the schedule that fixed it. There was no amount of working harder that would make me want to be there, and working harder was the only instrument I owned. I remember standing there thinking, is this what I signed up for? What I wanted, more than anything, was out. Not out of a rotation. Out.

Here is what I wrote about that period in the first edition of this book, in 2021, as close to verbatim as I can give it. I had said I just wanted to escape, and then:

> It was so bad that I remember, at one point, thinking, *I should just jump off the top floor of this parking garage and be out of it.* That was a dark moment. I've never had major depressive disorder. As a physician, I know all the diagnostic criteria for clinical depression. Looking back, I can say that it probably wasn't a true moment of suicidal ideation—I don't think I would have actually done it. But just because I wasn't in the kind of crisis that might have required admission to a psych ward doesn't mean it's something I can brush off when I think about that time in my life.

I have read that paragraph more times than I have read anything else I have written. I want to take it apart slowly, because in a book about high performers it may be the single most useful thing I have ever accidentally recorded.

Notice the structure. The thought arrives, stated plainly and without euphemism: I should jump off the top floor of this parking garage. Then, within one sentence, the qualification begins. It was a dark moment, which is true and also a way of putting a fence around it. Then the credential: I have never had major depressive disorder. Then the credential's credential: as a physician, I know all the diagnostic criteria. Then the verdict, delivered by the same man who is the subject of the case: it probably was not a true moment of suicidal ideation, because I do not think I would have actually done it. And then, to my slight credit, the retraction of part of the verdict: that does not mean I can brush it off.

Four sentences to reason my way from a specific plan involving a specific structure to a reassuring conclusion, using my medical training as the engine of the reassurance. If a colleague had handed me that paragraph about a patient, I would have circled it. I would have asked what "probably" was doing there. I would have pointed out that the assessment "I don't think I would have actually done it" is not an assessment; it is a hope, delivered in the voice of an expert.

Years later, I jumped from a parking garage.

I am not going to tell that story here. It has its own chapter, and it needs the room. What belongs in this chapter is only the connection between the two moments, because the connection is the whole reason I am rewriting this book rather than reprinting it.

Being intelligent does not make you objective about yourself. It gives you better tools for whatever you were already inclined to do, and if you are inclined to look away from something, intelligence will build you a more sophisticated way to look away. My medical knowledge did not protect me from a dangerous thought. It supplied the vocabulary that let me file the thought and move on. I knew the criteria, therefore I would know if I met them, therefore I did not meet them. Every link in that chain is broken, and I could not see one of them, because the chain was made of the same material as my competence.

There is a second thing hiding in that paragraph, and I missed it for years. Look at what I used as my standard: whether I was in the kind of crisis that would require admission. I was measuring myself against the threshold for hospitalization. Not against whether I was suffering. Not against whether a friend hearing me say it out loud would have been frightened. Against the highest bar in the system, the one where somebody takes your shoelaces. Anything below that bar counted as fine. That is a spectacularly high standard for fineness, and I suspect it is common among people who are used to operating in the top percentile of everything. If you have spent your life being graded against the best, you will grade your own suffering the same way, and you will find it unimpressive.

The honest thing to say about that year is that I do not know what it was. I am not going to diagnose the twenty-eight-year-old in the parking garage from here; retrospective diagnosis is unreliable even when you have the chart, and I do not have the chart, because there was no chart. What I can say is that he was in enough trouble to have that thought, that he told nobody, and that he decided the matter himself, competently, in four sentences.

Doctors are not spared this. The most careful recent look at the question pooled thirty-nine studies from twenty countries and found that suicide rates among male physicians were not measurably different from the general population, while among female physicians they were substantially higher, about seventy-six percent higher overall; rates have fallen over time, but in the most recent period female physicians still had roughly a twenty-four percent higher rate than the general population.[^c02-1] In the first edition I cited a much more dramatic figure that I had picked up somewhere and never chased down. I have removed it. If a claim in this book matters enough to affect what you think, it should be one I can point you to, and the real numbers are serious enough without embellishment.

* * *

What I did with my misery, in the end, was look for an exit that would not look like an exit.

The first one I considered was pathology. I emailed an old professor of mine from medical school, thinking that a specialty with no patients in it might be less taxing. It is not, for the record, an easier field; it is a demanding one with its own long training. But the people I spoke with told me their department regularly heard from burned-out residents in other specialties who were looking for a door. I did not actually want to be a pathologist. I wanted to stop.

Then I remembered that before I matched into plastics, I had seriously considered dermatology. The hours are good. The pay is good. You are far less likely to be woken up by an emergency that will define somebody's life by morning. What had pulled me away from it was a specific belief about what plastic surgery offered, which was the chance to change a person's life in an operating room.

That belief was not wrong. It was just imprecise, and precision turned out to matter. What I loved was not surgery in general. It was the geometry: designing a flap, working out how to move tissue from where it is to where it needs to be, closing something that a moment ago looked unclosable. In training I was learning an enormous range of operations, from cleft lip repair to hand surgery to breast reconstruction, and as an intern I had the rare privilege of putting my hands on and protecting a two-year-old's brain during a joint case with neurosurgery, reshaping a skull whose sutures had fused too early. I do not want to undersell what that felt like. But the more of it I did, the clearer it became that I did not want to do every kind of surgery. I wanted to master a few operations and do them very often and very well.

There is a chair of dermatology at Indiana named Steve Wolverton, whose name carries some weight in the field because he wrote a well-known pharmacology textbook, and because plastics and dermatology overlap, I had come to know him. One day I told him the truth: "I don't think that I'm happy in plastic surgery. I think I'm supposed to be a dermatologist." He took me under his wing. That is the entire mechanism by which my life changed direction, and I would like to point out how small it was. A sentence said out loud to one person who was in a position to help. I would spend the following decade unlearning that lesson, and then relearning it under much worse conditions.

He helped me into a newly opened dermatology spot at Howard University Hospital in Washington, DC, generously funded through the Veterans Affairs Medical Center. My salary there was low enough that I qualified for affordable housing, which is a strange thing to be able to say about a person in his thirties with two degrees. From there I came back to California for a fellowship in Mohs micrographic surgery, which is what I do now.

Mohs works like this. I remove a skin cancer, usually from a face, usually a nose or an ear or an eyelid, in a way that preserves a map of exactly where every edge of the specimen came from. Then I process the tissue and read the margins myself, under my own microscope, while the patient waits. If there is cancer left at an edge, the map tells me precisely where, and I go back for that spot and only that spot. When the margins are clear, I reconstruct the defect. It is the perfect hybrid of the two fields I had been torn between: the pathology and the surgery in one afternoon, the flaps I loved from plastics, restoring a nose's contour and its ability to do the unglamorous work of breathing. A bilateral DIEP flap for breast reconstruction can take twenty hours. My reconstructions take ten or twenty minutes. That fit my temperament exactly. A patient walks in with a cancer on her face and walks out the same day with it gone and the hole closed, and I get to go home.

Leaving plastic surgery was not a light decision, and I did not make it alone. Before I moved, I called plastic surgeons who were further along and asked whether they were happy, and whether, given the chance to do it again, they would make the same choices. Several were remarkably candid. One, Adam, was fifty-five. "I don't have a family," he told me. "I don't have a wife. I don't have kids. Going back, did I make the right decisions? I don't know." Another, Bill, was the father of a resident I had rotated with back in Michigan, and he had the family Adam did not. He said, "You know what, Patrick? I didn't spend a lot of time with my children when they were young, and so I feel like I missed out on them growing up. That is something that I regret."

Both of those conversations happened years before Adrian was born. I did not know what I was being told. I heard two accomplished men describing a bill that comes due much later, and I filed it as useful information about specialty selection, which is a bit like reading a warning label as a recipe. The part I did absorb, and I am grateful I absorbed it, was that they were telling me the truth when they had no obligation to. Somebody further up the ladder reached back down to a resident who was about to make a large decision, and told him something that made them look less impressive than they could have looked.

That is worth pausing on, because the training culture I have been describing does the opposite by default. It runs on scarcity: a limited number of spots, a curve to be beaten, a race in an operating room to answer first. Scarcity teaches people to hold on to what they know.

You have probably seen the crabs at a seafood counter or on a beach, and noticed that nobody bothers to put a lid on the barrel. There is no need for one. Every time a crab gets a little height, another crab reaches up and pulls him back down, and so none of them ever get out. Crabs do not know any better. People do, and mostly still do it, and I have been on both sides. I was a crab in that pediatrics rotation, and I was reached for by Steve Wolverton and Adam and Bill. Instead of behaving like crabs in a barrel, so determined to keep people beneath us, we might work together to get out of the damn barrel.

* * *

I changed specialties, and it worked. The exhaustion and the resentment did not follow me into dermatology. I built a practice, I found work I could do for thirty years without hating it, and the parking-garage thought receded into the category of things that had happened to me during training, along with the four a.m. rounds and the scorpion questions.

What I concluded from that success is the part I would revise. I decided that I had solved the problem. I had been unhappy, I had identified the cause, I had changed my circumstances, and the unhappiness went away, which meant the method was sound: diagnose, act, resolve. It also meant, in the private arithmetic I was not aware of doing, that choosing correctly once had made me safe. A man who has successfully changed his life at thirty tends to believe he is the kind of man who changes his life successfully.

There is one more thing I want to leave here before we move on. I had just spent six years chasing a destination that turned out to be wrong for me, and the first thing I did after escaping it was to start choosing new destinations, faster and with more ambition than before. It did not occur to me to ask whether the wanting itself was worth examining. I had upgraded the target. I had not touched the machinery.

That machinery has a name, and most of the ambitious people I know are running it. It is a bargain, and it is the subject of the next chapter.
