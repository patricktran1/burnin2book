# The Cost of Performing Fine

In the first edition of this book, in a chapter about mindset, I wrote this: "If you took away all my material possessions today, even if I went bankrupt, I would not be fazed. I know that I could get it back." The paragraph went on to say that I'd rebuild faster than I'd built, and that the one thing nobody could take from me was the way I thought. I wrote those sentences during the year my son was in treatment, and I meant them. That is the part worth paying attention to. I wasn't posturing for an audience. I'd just spent a year on the wrong side of medicine, watching what could and couldn't be taken from a person, and I had sorted money firmly into the first category and my own mind into the second. It was a good sentence. It was also a confident description of a test I'd never taken.

By then I'd also spent a couple of years in rooms I described, in that edition, with a pride I can now hear from across the room: people worth ten or a hundred times what I was worth, generous with their time, thinking at a scale I'd never been taught to think. I said earlier that I was getting ahead of the story. This is the story.

Several of the commercial real-estate transactions I had entered were put together by a man I trusted named Matt Onofrio. He pleaded guilty to bank fraud, and in December 2025 a federal judge in Minnesota sentenced him to three years in prison. The government's case described a scheme running from 2020 to 2022, sixty-eight transactions, and roughly four hundred and twenty million dollars in loans obtained fraudulently: purchase agreements assigned on to new investors at inflated prices, down-payment loans kept off the paperwork, buyers coached on what to tell the banks.[^c05-1]

I was on the buying side of that world. What I've never had is an honest accounting of my own part in it: which of the deals I signed were what they appeared to be on the day I signed them, what I should have seen and when, and how much of what came next was the transactions and how much was the speed I was moving at, which was mine. Nobody has audited that. I have my suspicions. They aren't flattering and they aren't evidence.

What happened to him is a matter of record and it isn't what this chapter is about. This chapter is about what the fear did to the man having it, because that is the part that nearly killed me, and it's the part I think you're most likely to recognize.

* * *

I believed I could be facing nearly ten million dollars in personal exposure. Nobody ever handed me a bill for ten million dollars. What I had was a set of documents I had signed, a growing list of things the attorneys didn't yet know, and a mind that treated every unknown as though it had already resolved in the worst available direction. Put those together at three in the morning and you get a number. The number was nearly ten million, and for a long time I couldn't hold anything else in my head.

I imagined bankruptcy, and I imagined it the way a resident imagines the complication he has just read about, in full procedural detail and with himself on the table. There was the filing. Then the practice I'd built, the house, the accounts, all of it itemized on a schedule for a stranger to dispose of. Worst was the conversation with my parents, who had left a war behind and built two careers in medicine and dentistry, in which I explained that their son had reached the top of his field and then lost it in a hurry to have more. My son's college fund I pictured in full, and then I pictured it gone. None of this had happened. All of it happened every night.

The odd thing is that bankruptcy itself was never the frightening part. Bankruptcy is a legal process. People go through it and come out the other side with their children still speaking to them and their hands still able to hold a scalpel. What frightened me was what losing everything would prove: that I'd been arrogant, that I had squandered what my parents' generation had crossed an ocean to give me, and that the degrees and the training and the summa cum laude and the steady hands were a long, expensive route to failure, and that everyone who had ever found me too confident had been right. Somewhere in the first weeks, without my noticing the moment, the problem changed category. It stopped being a problem about money. It became a problem about who I was, and I'd no procedure for that.

My first response was the only one I had, which was to outwork it. I read everything the attorneys sent and then read what they had cited. I made lists of what I could sell and in what order, and lists of people to call, and a schedule for the calls, because a schedule had gotten me through organic chemistry and a schedule had gotten me through residency and I'd never met a problem a schedule couldn't at least contain. This one ate the schedule. Every answer produced two questions, every document referred to another document, and the attorneys, who were good at their jobs, kept telling me with great care that they didn't yet know. A high performer can survive almost anything except not knowing. I'd built an entire personality on the premise that enough work converts uncertainty into a plan, and here was an uncertainty that wouldn't convert, and the machine, having nothing to grip, began to grind on the only material available, which was me.

I know how to hold a number at arm's length. In clinic, a lab value out of range is a fact, and what the fact means depends on the rest of the patient; that is most of what a medical education teaches you to do. I couldn't do it here. I couldn't stand far enough from this number to ask what else was true about the person attached to it. The number and I were the same size. When I looked at it I saw myself, and when I looked at myself I saw the number, and there was nothing in the training for that.

* * *

The attorneys were in the Midwest, where the working day begins before mine did, and so the crisis kept a schedule. I woke in the dark and took calls about my own possible ruin while the windows were still black. Those hours before dawn had once been the part of the day I liked best; I'd written about them in the first edition as the hours that belonged to me. Now they belonged to conference lines. There is a particular tone of voice that lawyers use when they're trying to be honest with you and kind to you at the same time, and I came to know it well, and I learned to answer it in a level voice, as if we were discussing someone else's case. Then the call would end, and I'd put on a shirt and drive to work and cut cancer out of people's faces.

I can give you the shape of one of those mornings, because for a long stretch they all had the same shape. A patient in her eighties or nineties, somebody's mother, somebody's grandmother, is sitting on the table with a skin cancer on the side of her nose and her daughter in the chair against the wall. At the consultation she may well have told me I look twelve; a fair number of them do. Now she is trusting me with her face. Mohs surgery is done in stages: I remove the visible cancer with a narrow margin, map it, and read the slides myself under the microscope while she waits with a bandage on, and if the edges aren't clear I go back for more. When they're clear I rebuild what I've taken, rotating a flap of neighboring skin into the defect so that a nose still looks like a nose. The reconstruction takes me ten or twenty minutes. My hands were steady. I want that on the record, because it's the whole point: my hands were steady all the way through. The daughter stops me in the hallway afterward to say thank you, sometimes in tears, and I say what I always say, that her mother did great, and I mean it, and I walk to the next room, where the next patient has been waiting and the phone in my pocket holds new messages from people whose hourly rate I don't want to think about.

Almost nobody knew. I say almost because a few people knew the deals were a mess, and one or two knew I wasn't sleeping, and the deals could be discussed in the vocabulary of business, which is a vocabulary built for discussing trouble without feeling any of it. What almost nobody understood was how bad it was inside, and the reason they didn't understand it's the subject of this chapter. There is a name for what I was doing, though I didn't have it at the time. I was performing fine. I don't mean that I was lying. The competence was real: the surgeries went well, the notes got finished, the staff got answers, the jokes in the hallway still landed. A person who is performing fine isn't pretending to be a surgeon. He is a surgeon. The performance isn't the skill. It's the use of the skill as evidence, presented to everyone including himself, that a man who can do this can't possibly be that sick.

I'd never have accepted that evidence from a patient. If a man sat in my clinic and told me he hadn't slept properly in weeks, that he woke every night in fear, that he thought constantly about ruin and had begun to think about not being here, and then assured me that he was fine because he was still going to work every day, the going to work wouldn't reassure me. It would alarm me. Function isn't a rule-out. I don't believe that depression in a person who keeps functioning is a milder illness; I believe it's the same illness in someone with more practice at concealment, and I'd have sent that man for help before he left the building. That man never got sent anywhere. I knew this material the way I know the layers of the skin, and I applied it to everyone but the one patient I saw every morning in the mirror while I shaved for the call.

Every profession has its version of the steady hands. For a founder it's the board meeting; for a lawyer, the closing; for an executive, the all-hands at which she says the quarter was hard and the team is strong. The performance is genuine in every case, which is what makes it dangerous, and the tell is never the performance itself. The tell is that it has become the only place the person still feels like himself. I felt like myself with a scalpel in my hand and nowhere else, and I arranged, without ever deciding to, to have a scalpel in my hand as often as possible.

Something else made the concealment easy, and it wasn't entirely inside me. Everyone around me had, in one way or another, hired me to be fine. My staff needed me fine so the schedule would run. The patients needed me fine so their faces would heal. And my attorneys, frankly, needed me fine, because a client who is coming apart is a harder client. The whole apparatus around a competent person is built to receive his competence, and it has no intake form for anything else. I don't say this to blame anyone. I say it because the first edition of this book put the entire problem inside the reader's head, and the years since have taught me that the outside participates.

The nights were the other half. I'd known fear before: the fear of a bad outcome in the operating room, the fear of a phone call about a child's blood counts. That kind of fear has a shape and an object. What arrived now didn't wait to be provoked. I'd wake at two or three with terror already present, fully formed, as if it had been sitting on the edge of the bed waiting for me to surface so that it could begin. There was nothing to think through, because I'd thought it all through the night before and the night before that; the thinking wasn't the point. Some part of my body had decided that the hours between midnight and the first legal call were for dread, and it kept that appointment more reliably than I kept any of mine. I could have drawn you the physiology on a napkin, the stress hormones and the nervous system locked in the posture it takes when the threat is in the room, and the drawing would have been correct and no help at all. Sleep went, first in pieces and then in the main. I did the things a physician knows to do and did them badly: I told myself to sleep, the way you might tell a wound to close.

The secrecy wasn't a decision. I never sat down and chose to hide it. It was more like a default set long ago, in an upbringing where children were seen and not heard and a training where you didn't bring trouble to work, and under load the default held. Someone would ask how I was and I'd say good, busy, which is the answer every physician gives every other physician and which no one in the history of the profession has ever followed up on. I told people the deals were a mess, which was true. I didn't tell anyone that I was waking in the dark unable to breathe and thinking, in a calm and reasonable and planning voice, about not existing. There is a chapter's worth to say about that silence and what it costs the people on the other side of it, and it's coming. Here I want only to point out that the silence and the competence were the same thing seen from two sides. The better I looked, the less anyone could reach me.

Two other things belong in this account, and I'll give them a sentence each, because they belong to what comes next more than to this. During this period I was also taking samples of a medication I prescribe, a drug whose label carries a warning I'd read many times on behalf of other people. And in the years before the crisis, in ceremonial settings, I'd used ayahuasca. I'll come back to both. I raise them now so that when I tell you what happened next, you'll know that I don't think it had a single cause, and that I don't think the search for a single cause is honest.

Here is something I didn't understand about depression before I had it, though I had recognized it in patients and referred it out for years. It's not persuaded by evidence. I had a son whose tumor I could still describe in centimeters, and I knew exactly what my absence would do to him. I could list every reason to stay, and on the worst nights I did list them, in the dark, like a checklist before a case. The list was correct and it did nothing. The illness doesn't lose the argument. It changes the rules so that there is no argument, only a conclusion that keeps arriving with the same terrible calm, and the more capable the mind hosting the conclusion, the more reasonable the conclusion sounds. A capable mind builds a very reasonable case, and mine did.

* * *

I went to the roof of an eight-story parking garage and jumped. I expected to die.

I landed in a bush. Everything around it was pavement. The branches took enough of the fall to leave me alive and not enough to leave me intact: bleeding inside my skull, bleeding into my liver and my spleen, my spine broken in several places. Any one of those kills people. I had all of them at once, and which ones I got was settled by where the branches happened to be.

A man standing where I stood believes he is choosing between two outcomes. He isn't choosing anything. People die falling down a flight of stairs. People fall two stories and never walk again. I fell four times that and lived. Not one of those outcomes was selected by the person it happened to.

I healed. That is the part I've never known what to do with. No limp, no scar a stranger would notice, nothing about me that reports any of it. The worst hour of my life is invisible on my body, the way the worst year of it was invisible in my clinic.

What I remember most clearly is the confusion. People assume that surviving an attempt on your own life produces relief, or gratitude, or a sudden clarity about what matters, and perhaps for some people it does. For me it produced something closer to bewilderment. I'd made a decision, the most final one a person can make, and it had been overruled by a bush. I was alive when I had arranged not to be, and I'd no procedure for that either. Nothing in the achievement machine covered it. In my whole life I'd never failed to finish something I had truly set out to do, and some cold, well-trained part of me registered this as a failure to finish before any warmer part could register it as anything else. I'm not proud of that reflex. I'm reporting it because I suspect it isn't unique to me, and because it tells you how far the machine will run on its own.

Years earlier, in surgical training, I'd stood in a parking garage and thought about jumping, and in the first edition I wrote that it probably wasn't real ideation, because I didn't think I'd actually have done it. I've already taken that sentence apart. I'll add only this: the resident who dismissed the thought and the man who acted on it were the same person, and the resident had been far surer of himself than he had any right to be.

* * *

Then there was a hospital, and people whose job it was to keep me alive, and after them, people whose job it was to ask why I hadn't wanted to be. I'd sat on the other side of those conversations. I'd been the one in the white coat asking the questions from the list, and I knew the list, and I can tell you now that knowing the list doesn't help you answer it. They were patient with me. The best of them worked the way I recognize from my own field: unhurried, exact, unimpressed by credentials, and unwilling to let me steer. I needed all four qualities, and I resented all four at the time, which I've since learned is close to diagnostic.

The treatment was treatment. It isn't a story, it's a schedule. You show up for it whether or not you yet believe in it, which is, as far as I can tell, most of how it works. What I can describe is the strangeness of being on the receiving end of the profession I'd spent my life inside. I'd thought of medicine as something I did. It turned out to be something that could be done to me, competently and without ceremony, by people who didn't care that I had once matched into plastic surgery, and I'm grateful now for exactly the indifference that offended me then.

There was also, and I haven't seen this written down often, the ordinariness. The world hadn't stopped. The deals were still a mess; the attorneys still needed answers; the sun still came up on schedule and brought the same list with it. I had expected, insofar as I had expected anything, that a thing this large would rearrange the furniture of reality, and instead reality had kept its furniture exactly where it was and simply put me back in the room. That was humbling in a way I hadn't been humbled before. The first edition talked a great deal about putting the ego down. This was the ego being put down for me, by circumstance, and it didn't feel like enlightenment. It felt like an ordinary weekday morning. Only much later could I see that this was the point.

I told a few people the truth. Who and how I'm going to leave out, because the who is theirs and the how comes later, but I'll say that the sentence itself, spoken out loud to someone who loved me, was harder than the roof. Never in my life had I said I can't do this and meant it. I'd said it about call schedules and organic chemistry and burn surgery, always as a preface to doing it anyway. This time there was no anyway. The people I told didn't fix anything, because there was nothing to fix in that sense. What they did instead was stay, and staying turned out to be the treatment they were qualified to give.

Recovery, when it came, wasn't an event. I kept waiting for the morning I'd wake up restored, the way I had woken up from every other bad stretch of my life ready to make a schedule and get to work. That morning never came. Recovery didn't arrive. It accumulated. A night with five hours of sleep instead of three. Then a morning when the terror was there but smaller, or there but later, or there and then gone by the time the coffee was done. One week I noticed, only afterward, that I'd felt ordinary dread on a legal call rather than the other kind. Nothing about it was cinematic and none of it announced itself, and if you had asked me at any point in those months whether I was better, I'd have said no and been half wrong. That, I understand now, is what recovery mostly looks like from the inside: nothing, for a long time, and then, in retrospect, a slope.

I experienced it, and still experience it, as though I'd been caught by the hand of God. I'm not going to explain that sentence, because I can't, and I distrust people who can. Take it as a report of an experience and not as a claim about how the universe is run; that is how I take it, most days, and on the other days I let it sit there unexplained, which is a skill I didn't have before.

If any of this is your present tense rather than your past, I want to say one practical thing before going on: in the United States you can call or text 988 at any hour and reach a person.[^c05-2] I'm putting the number here rather than only at the back of the book because this is the chapter it belongs in.

The cost of performing fine wasn't the roof. The roof was the last item on the bill. The cost was months in which the people who might have helped me were looking at a man who didn't appear to need help, because I'd made sure of it. It was the energy, too: it takes a great deal of effort to be excellent while coming apart, and every unit of that effort was effort I didn't spend getting well. And it was that I believed the performance myself. A surgeon who is operating well at eight in the morning has trouble taking seriously, at noon, the idea that he is in danger, and I had a surgical schedule that supplied fresh evidence of my own soundness every day of the week. The performance didn't hide the illness from other people, or not only. It hid it from me.

* * *

Years later, I saw the bush again. It was an ordinary bush, the kind nobody chooses on purpose. There was still a hollow in it where I had gone through, the branches never having closed back over the gap, and flowers were growing out of the hollow. In the space my body had made, I mean, not on the bush around it. I stood there for a while and didn't think anything I could put into a sentence. For now I'm going to leave the picture there, without telling you what it means, partly because you can see what it means and partly because I want to come back to it at the very end of this book, when I have earned the right to say more.

Let me end with a word. For most of my career I'd used it for everything that was wrong with doctors, and in the first edition of this book I used it about myself with some authority. Burnout is the word I'd have used for what happened to me, if I'd been describing it in someone else. It's the wrong word.
