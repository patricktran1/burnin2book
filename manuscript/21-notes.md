## Chapter 1

[^c01-1]: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is predominantly, though not exclusively, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Where it is, each *child* of an affected person has a fifty percent chance of inheriting the variant; risks to siblings and parents are assessed differently, and a grandchild's risk is conditional on the intervening parent having inherited it. Carrying a variant is also not the same as developing the disease, which is variably penetrant. Lopes LR, Ho CY, Elliott PM, "Genetics of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: established and emerging implications for clinical practice," *European Heart Journal* 2024;45(30):2727–2734, doi:10.1093/eurheartj/ehae421, https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/45/30/2727/7710314 (Introduction; Table 1; "Current clinical utility: diagnosis and family screening"). Cirino AL, Channaoui N, Ho C, "Nonsyndromic Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Overview," GeneReviews, updated 6 March 2025, "Autosomal Dominant Inheritance — Risk to Family Members," https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1768/. No family genetic testing result is asserted here.

## Chapter 2

[^c02-1]: Zimmermann C, Strohmaier S, Herkner H, Niederkrotenthaler T, Schernhammer E, "Suicide rates among physicians compared with the general population in studies from 20 countries: gender stratified systematic review and meta-analysis," *BMJ* 2024;386:e078964, published 21 August 2024, doi:10.1136/bmj-2023-078964, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39168499/. Thirty-nine studies from twenty countries; pooled suicide rate ratios of 1.05 (95% CI 0.90–1.22) for male physicians and 1.76 (1.40–2.21) for female physicians, each compared with the same sex in the general population. The confidence interval for male physicians crosses one, which means the difference was not statistically significant; that is not the same as showing the rates are equal. The 24 percent excess for female physicians is the result for the ten most recent studies rather than for a defined range of calendar years.

## Chapter 6

[^c06-1]: World Health Organization, "Burn-out an 'occupational phenomenon': International Classification of Diseases," news release, 28 May 2019, https://www.who.int/news/item/28-05-2019-burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon-international-classification-of-diseases. ICD-11 classifies burn-out as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition, defines it as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed, gives the three dimensions quoted here, and states that the term should not be applied to experiences outside the occupational context.
